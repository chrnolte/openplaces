Methods

How the land and structure value pages are made, and where they can be wrong.

Heights and colors

Every lot and every building stands as high as its value per unit of ground: a lot by its land value per acre, a building by its value per square foot of its outline. Tall shapes are where the most value sits on the least ground. Colors follow the same values on a logarithmic scale, so that a doubling of value looks alike at any price level.

"Value per home" recolors homes by their value divided by their number of homes. A home is one house, apartment or condo, so an apartment building's value is divided among its apartments. Lots and buildings that hold no homes keep a color by value per ground area, on the second, thinner strip of the legend.

Values

The values are those the local tax assessors recorded; each page's Sources panel names them, with their terms. Where a roll leaves a value out, openplaces fills it in. "Imputed" shows the recorded values with these fills, and the tooltip marks a filled value as estimated. "Assessment" shows the recorded values alone and draws filled ones as no value. A lot or building the assessor values at $0, often public or exempt land, is drawn white; one with no recorded value at all is drawn gray.

Unexplained buildings

A building is drawn as high as its value per square foot of outline. A tall building carries a high value per square foot of outline because it has many floors. Some outlines carry a value per square foot that no measured height explains. They would stand as needles that no building on the ground matches. We mark them, and they are hidden unless you switch on the button with the question mark, which draws them see-through.

An outline is marked unexplained when its value comes to more than $3,500 per square foot of outline and, divided by its measured floors, still to more than $3,000 per square foot of floor. Floors are the story count that Overture Maps records for the outline, or its measured height divided by 3.5 m. An outline with neither counts as one floor.

For comparison, across Boston, Cambridge, Somerville and Everett the median outline comes to about $210 per square foot of floor. The usual causes of an unexplained building are:

  • the value belongs to more than the outline holds: a lot's other buildings, or a structure that the outlines we draw do not include;
  • the outline has no measured height, so a genuinely tall building counts as one floor.

The value itself is not changed. The mark says only that we cannot confirm the height we would draw.

Values our model estimated for a building get the same check.

Lots are marked too. A lot is drawn as high as its land value per acre, and a few tiny outlines, slivers or air-rights parcels, carry the value of a whole account.

A lot is marked unexplained when it is smaller than 100 square meters and its land value per acre is more than ten times the value that only one lot in a thousand on the page exceeds.

Extent

Each page shows everything whose center lies within 15 km of a fixed point: Boston University's Marsh Plaza for Boston, the city hall elsewhere. Lots and buildings are kept whole, never cut at the circle. Phones get a lighter page with a 7 km circle around the same point.

Known issues

We look for our own mistakes. Each page lists the problems we know of at the top of its Sources panel, with the date we found each one, until the fix is live.
